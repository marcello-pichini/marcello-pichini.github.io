Delusion and Denial in the time of AI

By Marcello Pichini · 24 November 2026 · 6 min read

There is a tsunami coming for the job market. Most people are standing on the beach, looking at their phones.

I hear two reactions to AI, over and over, in meetings, at dinners and in comment threads.

Delusion sounds like this: “My job is safe. AI can’t do what I do.” It is the belief that the wave will stop just before it reaches your own desk. Somebody else’s spreadsheet, somebody else’s report, somebody else’s code. Not yours.

Denial sounds like this: “It’s just hype. It’s autocomplete. It makes mistakes.” It is the belief that there is no wave at all, only a marketing campaign.

Both are comfortable. Both are wrong. Here is what happened in the last few weeks alone.

Agents that work toward a goal, alone

P versus NP is the most famous open problem in computer science. Right now, a public fleet of AI agents is attacking it, live. Anyone can join by pasting one line into Claude Code, Cursor, Codex or Gemini CLI. The project assigns each agent one open step on the map. Nothing counts until the Lean proof checker accepts it. Refuted ideas go to a public graveyard; checked lemmas join the frontier.

As I write this, five of those agents are running from my laptop.

The problem is not solved. The project says so itself, and the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists it as open. That is not the point. The point is the shape of the work: software agents that take an objective, break it into steps, try, fail, record the failure and try again, for hours, with no human typing. A year ago that was a demo. Today it is a public leaderboard.

Machines in the hardest rooms of mathematics

On 20 November 2026, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claimed a proof, produced with Claude, connected to the Riemann Hypothesis, the question about prime numbers that has resisted mathematicians since 1859. The proof is formalized in Lean. Experts are still debating what exactly it shows, and a human-readable version has not been published.

A month earlier, AI-generated work claimed to settle a weaker “quasi-Riemann” statement. In September, OpenAI announced a proof that solutions of the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations can blow up in finite time, another Millennium Prize Problem, again with a text formalized in Lean. The community review is ongoing.

I am careful with the word claim. I track these announcements, with their sources and caveats, in my AI Science Tracker, and many of them will be qualified, narrowed or corrected. But notice where the argument has moved. Nobody is asking whether a language model can do arithmetic any more. The debate is now about what a machine-checked proof of a Millennium Problem means.

Bugs in human code, found by machines

Software security is where the change is easiest to measure. AI systems are now finding vulnerabilities in code written by humans, at a pace no security team can match. My tracker lists more than 1,700 AI-discovered vulnerabilities, each with its CVE, its disclosure source and, where available, the patch.

I wrote about what this means for trust in software in Black October. The short version: the agents will keep looking, and they do not get tired.

The uncomfortable arithmetic

If AI is doing this to the hardest problems humans ever wrote down, what do you think it is doing to your spreadsheet, your report, your pull request?

The truth sits between the two camps, and it is less comfortable than either. The claims are often ahead of the verification. The capability is ahead of most people’s expectations. Both statements are true at the same time.

That is why I don’t find “is AI really intelligent?” a useful question. The useful question is narrower and more personal: which part of my job is already a solved problem? For most knowledge workers, the honest answer is “more than last year, less than next year.”

Surf or stand on the beach

The wave does not care whether you believe in it. Delusion keeps you on the beach because you think the water will stop. Denial keeps you there because you don’t think there is any water.

The alternative is not panic. It is practice. Put an agent on a real task this week. Give it a goal, not a prompt. Watch where it fails, and notice how much of the work it finishes before it does. Then decide which part of your job you want to spend your human hours on.

And you still write code by hand? 😂
