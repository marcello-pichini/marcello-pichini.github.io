When agents outnumber shoppers, who handles the payments?

By Marcello Pichini · 2 October 2026 · 12 min read

You buy a report once. Your agent might buy the data, the computation, the verification and the right to read fifty sources before it writes that report. The bill may stay small. The number of payments may not.

We built online checkout around a person making a decision. An agent can make hundreds of smaller decisions while that person is asleep. If software becomes the most frequent customer on the internet, payment infrastructure will need to serve a very different pattern of demand.

Does that favour Bitcoin, Ethereum, Visa or Kaspa? Before picking a winner, we need to know what we are asking them to carry.

The web analogy is useful—with one correction

The web was never entirely human. Search crawlers, scripts and bots have been around for decades. What is changing is the role of software: from indexing a page to selecting a service, negotiating access and potentially paying for it.

In its July 2026 agentic-internet report, Cloudflare says non-human traffic has passed 50%. That is a traffic measure from a provider’s perspective, not a census of independent AI agents. Crawlers are not all buyers, and ten thousand requests can still end in one purchase.

The same caution belongs in payments. More agents than people is plausible as a scenario: one person or business can operate many agents. More agent-initiated payments than human-initiated payments is a separate hypothesis. There is no reliable global count establishing that crossover today.

Start with a baseline we can defend

McKinsey’s 2026 Global Payments Report describes a market of approximately 3.8 trillion annual transactions. Spread over a 365-day year, that is about 10.4 billion a day, or 120,500 a second.

That broad industry estimate includes business and already automated flows. I use it as a proxy for the existing economy—not as a measured total of people clicking “pay.” It is also not a count of card purchases alone.

For a narrower, company-reported reference, Visa reported 257.5 billion processed transactions in fiscal 2025, including payment and cash transactions. That averages roughly 8,165 a second. Visa is a subset of the wider market; adding those two figures would double-count activity. Visa’s reported results are preferable here to loosely defined marketing totals.

Two futures that look similar—and scale differently

In the first future, an agent orders your groceries instead of you. One checkout replaces another. The payment network gains a new interface and a new authorization problem, but not necessarily another transaction.

In the second, an agent buys individual units of useful work: a fresh market-data query, a document extraction, a forecast, a verification step or a second opinion from another agent. These are additional paid events. Breaking a monthly subscription into thousands of purchases could increase transaction counts without increasing the total bill.

The scenarios below model that second future. Every event is an additional chargeable event requiring one payment if settled individually. Free requests, delegated human checkouts and internal model calls are excluded. There is no target date attached to these adoption levels.

In the middle scenario, agents generate about 90.6% of payment events. It takes only a billion active agents making a hundred paid calls a day to get there—one paid call roughly every fourteen minutes. That sounds modest at the agent level and enormous at the network level.

But the economic constraint arrives before the spectacular TPS number. A hundred billion daily purchases at $0.001 each imply $100 million of daily spending. At $0.10, the same activity costs $10 billion a day. Demand needs a budget and a useful result, not just a loop that keeps running.

Change the assumptions yourself

Four names, four different jobs

Comparing “TPS” without defining the transaction can be badly misleading. A card authorization message, a confirmed Bitcoin transaction, an Ethereum token transfer and a rollup operation do not represent the same work or the same point in settlement.

For the base chains, I use transparent arithmetic rather than a familiar headline number. The following are simplified resource-budget examples. They ignore existing demand, reserve capacity, block overhead and some other constraints; they are not load-test results.

Bitcoin: the payment layer matters more than the L1 headline

Bitcoin’s base chain cannot plausibly record every agent purchase in these scenarios. The illustration uses the 4-million-unit block-weight limit and the roughly ten-minute block interval. Transaction size and output batching change how many actual payments fit.

Lightning takes a different approach: payments move through channels and use Bitcoin for funding and settlement. It is a credible candidate for frequent small BTC-denominated payments. Its constraints move to routing, inbound and outbound liquidity, channel management and reliable availability. Lightning Labs’ liquidity documentation explains why having enough funds overall does not guarantee the right channel can receive or forward a payment.

There is no single network-wide Lightning TPS limit that proves it can carry our stress case. The BTC exchange-rate exposure also remains unless another service handles conversion. High payment frequency does not remove those operational choices.

Ethereum: execution moves to rollups

The L1 illustration follows Ethereum’s documented block configuration and 21,000-gas plain ETH transfer. Stablecoin transfers and smart-wallet policy checks do more work; that ETH-transfer calculation is not their TPS.

Ethereum’s rollup-based scaling approach separates high-volume execution from base-layer settlement and data availability. That makes Ethereum plus L2s a plausible platform for programmable stablecoin payments, spending policies and agent wallets. It does not give the ecosystem an unlimited shared throughput budget.

The difficult parts include moving liquidity between rollups, sequencer availability, proof generation, data costs and withdrawal or finality assumptions. Each deployment needs its own workload test. Adding advertised TPS across unrelated chains does not demonstrate a seamless global payment service.

Visa: a checkout agent does not require a new currency

Visa already has distribution, issuer relationships and a framework for managing disputes. Its Intelligent Commerce offering describes agent credentials, controls and authentication, while noting that deployment is still in progress and final features may differ.

A 2025 Visa infrastructure article cites up to 83,000 transaction messages per second. That is neither its annual average payment count nor proof of 83,000 fully settled purchases every second indefinitely. Messages per payment, issuer and acquirer capacity, peak demand and downstream settlement all matter.

Visa is a strong fit for agents buying ordinary goods from established merchants. One card charge for every fraction-of-a-cent API call is a different economic proposition. Aggregated billing can make that workload more sensible without replacing the card network.

Kaspa: faster consensus still needs a payment ecosystem

Kaspa’s blockDAG takes a different route to proof-of-work throughput. Its current project description reports 10 blocks per second and the June 2026 Toccata upgrade, including covenant-based programmability. Describing it simply as a chain with no programmability would now be out of date.

The 2,500/s illustration comes from the mainnet resource parameters and an explicitly assumed compute mass per transfer. Storage mass, scripts, transaction structure and practical node performance can reduce it. Ten blocks per second is not ten transactions per second, and neither figure proves finality for a particular payment risk.

Kaspa merits testing for low-value native transfers. A broader agent-payment system would also need dependable wallets, merchant access, fiat conversion and, where required, mature stable-value assets and liquidity. Programmability enables possibilities; it does not establish production adoption of each one.

The scalable solution may involve fewer settlements

The most consequential number may be the ratio between paid events and external settlements. In the middle scenario, the agents alone create about 1.16 million paid events a second. Net a hundred eligible events into each settlement and that falls to about 11,574 external transfers a second. At a thousand, it is roughly 1,157.

A service can meter usage internally and charge periodically. Recurring counterparties can use channels. Rollups can batch execution and publish the required data and proofs. These mechanisms reduce different kinds of work; they are not interchangeable, and none makes recordkeeping disappear.

Protocols such as x402 make payment negotiation usable by software through HTTP 402. They do not create a new consensus network. A protocol can be easy for an agent to use while the underlying rail still faces liquidity, fee or capacity constraints.

Nor should a protocol dashboard be mistaken for a census of genuine autonomous commerce. A 2026 measurement paper on x402 specifically examines adoption and authenticity. We need to distinguish paid demand for useful services from synthetic activity, incentives and ordinary software relabelled as agents.

The hurdles a faster chain cannot solve

Authority. An agent should spend within a revocable mandate: approved counterparties, a per-purchase ceiling, a daily budget and an expiry. The system signing the payment should enforce those constraints independently of the model’s prose. A malicious page must not be able to turn “summarize this report” into “send your balance here.”

Payment correctness. Timeouts and retries must not create duplicate charges. The receipt needs to identify what was bought, by whom, under which permission and with what evidence of delivery. A confirmed transfer proves money moved; it does not prove the service was useful or even delivered.

Economics. On a $0.001 purchase, a $0.0001 all-in cost is already 10%. A $0.01 cost is 1,000%. Those are arithmetic examples, not current network fee quotes. Signing, fraud checks, reconciliation and failed attempts cost money even when the chain fee is low.

Liquidity and denomination. Agents paying bills priced in dollars need predictable spending power. Stablecoins can help with denomination, but create issuer, reserve, redemption and chain risks. Circle’s reserve disclosures, for example, describe the backing of USDC; they do not remove operational risk from every wallet, bridge or venue using it.

Accountability. The agent is not a substitute for the responsible person or business. Compliance obligations, consumer protections, refunds and dispute handling depend on the service and jurisdiction. FATF’s virtual-asset update illustrates that adopting crypto rails does not make those obligations disappear.

Resilience. Agents can synchronize. A market event or a popular release could trigger a burst of purchases, failed calls and retries. Capacity planning needs headroom, backpressure and spending circuit breakers. Average TPS is the start of that exercise, not the result.

The likely winner is a stack

My working assumption is that agents will become a large source of payment instructions, but the architecture will remain plural. Cards and bank rails can serve retail purchases and consolidated invoices. Lightning can serve channel-based BTC payments. Stablecoins on suitable rollups or other networks can serve programmatic services. Kaspa can compete where its native transfers and growing application layer meet the actual requirements.

The strongest design separates the agent’s permission to spend, the unit in which prices are expressed, the execution rail and the final settlement. That lets us route a grocery order differently from a thousand paid data queries.

If agents become the majority of buyers by transaction count, the question will be less “Which coin is fastest?” and more “How much useful commerce can this entire system complete, safely and cheaply?” The internet may gain billions of new customers without gaining billions of new people. We should build payment systems for the difference.
